The physics

Stars and gas are treated as two continuous fluids in the galactic plane (face-on view), each with surface density Σ(x, t) and velocity v(x, t). They obey the compressible Euler equations:

∂Σ/∂t + ∇·(Σv) = 0
∂v/∂t + (v·∇)v = −∇P/Σ − ∇Φ

The pressure P = K Σγ stands in for the random motions of stars (velocity dispersion σ, P ≈ Σσ²) and for gas pressure (isothermal, cs ≈ 10 km/s).

Gravity comes from the Poisson equation with all the mass:

∇²Φ = 4πG (ρstars + ρgas + ρbulge + ρDM)

The disk's own part is solved with FFTs on the grid (a thin sheet with softening ε ≈ disk thickness); the bulge and the dark-matter halo are fixed spherical potentials:

NFW: ρ = ρs / [(r/rs)(1 + r/rs)²]
cored: ρ = ρ0 / [1 + (r/rc)²]

Why flat rotation curves imply dark matter. A circular orbit needs vc² = G M(<R)/R. Starlight is concentrated in the inner few kpc, so beyond it the visible mass predicts a Keplerian fall-off vc ∝ R−½ (orange curve). Real galaxies instead show flat curves out to tens of kpc, so M(<R) must keep growing ∝ R: an unseen halo with ρ ∝ r−2 (violet). Switch the halo off and watch the fluid rotation (dots) and the total curve (white) drop.

Spiral arms. A disk's stability is set by Toomre's Q = c κ / (π G Σ) (κ = epicyclic frequency). For Q ≲ 1.5 self-gravity swing-amplifies perturbations into trailing arms; for Q < 1 the disk fragments; hot disks (Q ≳ 2) stay smooth like S0 galaxies. A rotating bar or spiral potential drives a grand-design pattern; the gas responds most strongly and shocks along the arms.

"Normal" galaxies here are ellipticals (pressure-supported, little rotation) and lenticulars (hot rotating disks without arms).
